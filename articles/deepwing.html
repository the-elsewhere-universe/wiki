One of Elsewhere’s largest known animals, the Deepwing moves through the ocean less like a whale than an immense bird soaring through moving air.

Built for currents

Deepwings are unmistakably mammalian: dark charcoal above, mottled pale grey beneath, with a long, gently downturned head and expandable throat structures for engulfing plankton-like organisms and dense Silverfin shoals.

The defining anatomy is a pair of exceptionally long, flexible pectoral hydrofoils. Fully extended, their span can exceed the animal’s body length. A Deepwing banks, turns, glides and spirals through the currents, sometimes travelling far with little movement of its tail.

The Rising

During the autumn migration, Deepwings ascend from hundreds of metres below to feed near the surface at dusk. They spiral upward on currents flowing along Orra’s submerged basalt escarpment.

From a boat, pale shapes appear one by one beneath the dark water—until a thirty-five-metre animal passes almost silently below, its wings extending beyond either side.

This event is known as the Rising, one of Orra’s defining natural spectacles.

The cliffs are singing

Deepwings communicate through powerful infrasonic calls. When dozens gather offshore, those frequencies travel into the coastal rock. Windows tremble, water ripples in glasses and old wooden buildings creak.

Long before the cause was understood, Orrans knew that on certain autumn nights the cliffs themselves seemed to hum. The traditional expression remains: “The cliffs are singing.”

Fleets that never dock

Near the surface, a resting Deepwing may roll and lift one hydrofoil into the wind. It offers little propulsion, but helps orient the animal in the current. From distant cliffs the fin resembles a dark sail, giving rise to old stories of fleets that never dock.

Deepwings are now protected. Their arrival draws families to cliff viewpoints, research boats to sea and schools into the field. Their migration is both ecological event and shared cultural season.
