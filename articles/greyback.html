Each spring, Greybacks leave the sea and haul their immense bodies hundreds of metres up Orra’s escarpments to ancestral breeding grounds on the high plateau.

Grace in the wrong place

A Greyback resembles an enormous, heavily built otter more than a seal: dense slate-grey fur, a broad chest, thick neck and extraordinarily powerful shoulders. Five hooked claws and textured gripping pads find purchase on wet basalt; mobile rear limbs rotate beneath the body to bear its weight.

In water they are graceful, powerful swimmers. On flat ground they lumber. On steep rock they become extraordinary.

The Climb

Thousands emerge from the ocean in spring and begin ascending waterfall-cut gullies, narrow ledges and near-vertical faces. They do not adhere magically: every movement involves searching for holds, hauling upward and resting wherever the rock permits.

Adults sometimes sleep on shelves halfway up the escarpment, suspended between the sea they have left and the grasslands they are trying to reach.

Born above the ocean

Females give birth on sheltered grassy plateaus. For their first months, Greyback pups are effectively terrestrial animals—playing in long grass with the ocean hundreds of metres below.

Later they must make the reverse journey. The first descent of the young is among Orra’s most perilous seasonal passages.

A life across boundaries

Greybacks feed heavily on seasonal Silverfin shoals before climbing. Adults have few natural predators, but the young are vulnerable to an apex predator whose identity remains deliberately unresolved in the current canon.

The Greyback makes Orra’s governing ecological idea visible: here, life repeatedly crosses the boundaries between sea, land and cliff.
